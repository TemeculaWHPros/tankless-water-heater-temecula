How a tankless unit actually works

When a hot tap opens, a flow sensor wakes the unit, the burner fires, and cold water passes through a heat exchanger that brings it up to temperature in seconds. When the tap closes, the burner shuts off. There is no stored hot water, so there is nothing to keep warm and nothing to run out — but there is also no buffer. The unit can only heat as much water per minute as its burner and heat exchanger allow, which is why sizing matters more with tankless than with any other heater. A unit rated for nine gallons a minute will run two showers and a dishwasher at once; the same unit in a home with three bathrooms running simultaneously will leave someone lukewarm.

The most common tankless repairs

Almost every tankless repair call in the Temecula area falls into a small set of causes. Scale build-up in the heat exchanger is first: Temecula's hard water deposits minerals inside the narrow passages, the unit overheats, and it shuts itself down or throws an error code. A professional descale — circulating a cleaning solution through the exchanger — usually restores it fully. Second is ignition trouble: a dirty flame sensor, a failing igniter, or a gas supply that cannot keep up when the burner demands full fire. Third is the cold-water sandwich, where the water goes hot, then cold, then hot again at the start of a shower — often a sign of a recirculation or minimum-flow issue rather than a fault. And fourth is venting: a partially blocked or incorrectly sized vent can trip the unit's safety sensors on the coldest mornings.

Most of these are same-day fixes, and a licensed technician can diagnose them from the error code and a look at the installation. What a proper tankless installation includes

Swapping a tank for a tankless unit is not a like-for-like job, and the quote should reflect that honestly. The gas line often needs upsizing, because a tankless burner can demand three to four times the gas flow of a tank heater at full fire. The venting is different too: most modern units use sealed, direct-vent piping rather than the open flue a tank used, and condensate drainage has to be routed somewhere safe. On the water side, the unit needs isolation valves and service ports fitted at install time, so descaling is a simple job later rather than a plumbing project. A good installer also fits a scale filter or a water treatment device, because in hard-water country that one addition can double the time between descaling visits.

Sizing: the step that decides everything

Tankless sizing is a flow-rate calculation, not a guess. The technician adds up the fixtures likely to run at the same time — a shower uses roughly two to two-and-a-half gallons a minute, a dishwasher about one and a half, a washing machine about two — and then checks the temperature rise the unit must deliver. In winter, incoming water in Temecula can arrive at around 55 degrees, and the unit must lift it to about 120, a rise of 65 degrees. A unit that delivers nine gallons a minute at a 35-degree rise may only deliver five at a 65-degree rise, and the spec sheet does not always make that obvious. Getting this number right at install time is what separates a household that loves its tankless heater from one that complains about it for a decade.

Descaling and annual maintenance

In soft-water towns a tankless unit can go years without attention. In Temecula it cannot. The standard advice is a descale every twelve months, and twice a year for larger households or homes with very hard water. The job takes about an hour: the unit is isolated, a pump circulates a food-safe descaling solution through the heat exchanger, the inlet filter is cleaned, and the unit is tested at full fire. Skipping it is the single most common reason a tankless unit fails early — scale insulates the exchanger, the burner runs hotter, the sensors trip more often, and eventually a component gives up. Tankless or tank: an honest comparison

A tankless unit costs more up front — the unit, the gas-line work and the venting add up — and it pays that back over years of lower running costs and a longer life. It wins clearly for households that use a lot of hot water in bursts, for homes where the tank is in a tight space, and for anyone who has ever had a tank leak forty gallons onto the floor. A tank still wins for a small household on a tight budget, for homes where the gas supply cannot be upgraded cheaply, and for anyone who values the simplest possible system.
